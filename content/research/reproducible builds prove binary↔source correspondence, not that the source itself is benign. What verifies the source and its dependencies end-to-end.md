## Question

What — if anything — verifies that a program's **source** and its transitive **dependencies** are *benign*, given that [[Reproducible builds]] establishes only that a shipped binary corresponds to *some* source? Reproducible builds answer one question — "does this binary match this source?" (build integrity, i.e. binary↔source correspondence) — and are silent on another — "is this source trustworthy?" (origin + intent). This report asks what closes the gap between those two questions, and what anchors trust at the root of the dependency chain. Scope: free/open-source, publicly-auditable supply chains for general-purpose computing (Linux distributions, language/package ecosystems), restricted to mechanisms that are themselves inspectable. Hardware/firmware root-of-trust is explicitly out of scope and recorded only as residual trust.

## Why it matters

The question sits on a live tension the wiki keeps surfacing: [[Reproducible builds]] is frequently misread as a security proof when it is only a *correspondence* proof, and the end-to-end chain — source review → provenance → build integrity → compiler bootstrap — is currently unlinked into a single trust stack. Ken Thompson already drew the boundary in 1984: a compiler can be subverted so that a backdoor appears in *no source anywhere*, so "no amount of source-level verification" protects you from untrusted binaries (https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rdriley/487/papers/Thompson_1984_ReflectionsonTrustingTrust.pdf). Every answer to the question therefore has to name, for each layer, exactly *what invariant it proves* and *where its trust anchor sits* — and to say honestly whether those anchors compose into one transitive proof or instead rest on diversity of verifiers. Resolving this matters now for two reasons: the canonical incidents of the last five years (SolarWinds, XZ Utils) each exploited precisely one of these layers while the others held, and the cost of the answer is paid directly in the wiki's own concepts — [[Deterministic builds]], [[Bootstrappable builds]], [[Diverse double compilation]], [[Software provenance]], [[Software supply chain]], [[Cryptographic hash function]], and [[SLSA]] — which this report is the first card to connect into a single composition.

## Survey

**Thompson establishes the problem, not just an attack.** *Reflections on Trusting Trust* (https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rdriley/487/papers/Thompson_1984_ReflectionsonTrustingTrust.pdf) shows in three stages how a modified C compiler can (1) miscompile `login` to accept a secret password, and (2) re-insert both that backdoor and its own backdoor into every future compilation of itself, so that "the login command will remain bugged with no trace in source anywhere." His moral is the ground truth this report elaborates: "You can't trust code that you did not totally create yourself… No amount of source-level verification or scrutiny will protect you from using untrusted code," and he locates the residual risk downward — assembler, loader, "even hardware microcode," where "a well-installed microcode bug will be almost impossible to detect." Thompson's framing is precisely the distinction in the question: the *correspondence* between a binary and its source cannot be established by reading source, and the *benignity* of source is ultimately a statement about the people who wrote it, not about the text.

**Wheeler shows correspondence *can* be mechanically proved — and that proving it is not proving benignity.** Diverse Double-Compiling (https://dwheeler.com/trusting-trust) compiles a compiler's source twice: once with a second, trusted compiler using the source of the compiler's parent, then recompiles the compiler source with the result of that first compilation; if the output is bit-for-bit identical to the untrusted executable, "the source code accurately represents the executable." Wheeler's dissertation offers a formal proof that DDC determines source↔binary correspondence, demonstrated on four compilers including GCC and a deliberately malicious one. Three boundaries matter here. First, DDC does **not** assume two compilers produce identical output — the trusted compiler may even target a different CPU — so it is a genuine detection technique, not N-version programming. Second, DDC explicitly does **not** establish benignity: passing the test only means you can read the compiler's source to see what it does, and it cannot flag a telemetry insertion that is *present in the source*. Third, DDC still requires *one* trusted compiler to start, and rests its guarantee on the claim that an attacker must now subvert *multiple* compilers the defender may choose *after* the attack. So DDC converts "trust one opaque compiler binary" into "trust one compiler + hope attackers cannot predict which diverse compilers the verifier will use" — a [[Diverse double compilation]] anchor, but still an anchor. Wheeler's own page names GNU Mes as the real-world application of DDC, bridging this work to the bootstrap lineage below.

**Reproducible builds prove correspondence and disclaim benignity in the same breath.** The project's own definition is scoped precisely to the question's first clause: reproducible builds "verify that the binaries that you download match the original, untampered source code" ([[Reproducible builds (reproducible-builds.org)]]) — a binary↔source statement with no claim about the source itself. The independent-verifier paper from Lucerne/ETH (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2505.21642, arXiv:2505.21642) is the sharpest statement of the boundary: reproducible builds give "an independently-verifiable path from source to binary code," yet "R-B is not about making software more secure, insecure software will remain insecure." SLSA's FAQ says the same thing from the other side: reproducible builds "do not address source, dependency, or distribution threats," and even *verified* reproducible builds (two or more independent platforms corroborating provenance) are "not a complete solution to supply chain integrity" (https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/faq).

**The provenance layer verifies *origin and process*, not benignity.** SLSA splits provenance into two tracks: build provenance (artifact → source) and source provenance (revision → change-management process) (https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/provenance). The Source track's crown requirement — two-party review at L3, plus immutable revisions, protected refs, authenticated history — is an attestation that *two trusted persons approved a change through a controlled process*, not that the change is harmless (https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/source-requirements). Crucially, SLSA is explicitly **non-transitive**: "SLSA Build levels only cover the trustworthiness of a single build, with no requirements about the build levels of transitive dependencies" (https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/faq), and its threat model lists "dependency threats" as a category it "does not explicitly address" in this version — including the exact Thompson-shaped threat of "use a compromised build tool (compiler)" (https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/threats). Sigstore occupies the same layer with a different anchor: Fulcio mints short-lived certificates bound to an OIDC identity, Rekor records the signing event in an append-only log, and verification establishes "that someone with control over [an] identity signed for an artifact at a specific time" (https://blog.trailofbits.com/2022/11/08/sigstore-code-signing-verification-software-supply-chain). The identity anchor is centralized in the IdPs (GitHub, Google, Microsoft) and the Fulcio root; the guarantee is about *who and when*, explicitly not about whether what they signed is safe — Trail of Bits flags the open UX problem that users must understand "what exactly they're doing when they verify an identity's signature" (https://github.com/sigstore/docs/blob/main/content/en/about/overview.md).

**The bootstrap layer shrinks the opaque seed but does not eliminate it.** The Guix full-source bootstrap (https://guix.gnu.org/en/blog/2023/the-full-source-bootstrap-building-from-source-all-the-way-down) now roots a 22,000-node package graph in a **357-byte** hex0-seed, walking hex0 → hex1 → catm → hex2 → M0 → cc_x86 → M1 → M2 → M2-Planet → Mes → TinyCC → GCC 2.95 → GCC 4.7 → a modern toolchain. The Guix authors frame Thompson directly: the trusting-trust attack is "only a symptom of an incomplete or missing bootstrap story." Yet the residual is nonzero and named honestly: a still-present ~25 MiB statically-linked `guile-bootstrap` driver, an x86-only path (ARM/RISC-V "joining soon"), and beneath even the 357-byte seed, unverifiable firmware and hardware. The [[Bootstrappable builds]] project's goal — "to reduce the number and size of binary seeds to a bare minimum" (https://www.bootstrappable.org/projects/mes.html) — is minimization, not elimination: Mes is ~5,000 lines of C plus a Scheme C compiler, and stage0 begins from a 280-byte hex monitor. The point of the 357 bytes is that they are *humanly auditable* — hex0 is "readable as source" — so the seed is not an unauditable oracle but the smallest artifact a person can actually inspect.

**The two canonical incidents map cleanly onto the layer model.** SolarWinds/SUNBURST: SUNSPOT malware ran on Orion's *build servers*, watched for `MsBuild.exe`, replaced `InventoryManager.cs` with a malicious variant at compile time, then restored it — so "the codebase remained clean, while the compiled code was signed with the valid SolarWinds software certificate and shipped with the SUNBURST backdoor" (https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2023/solarwinds-explainer.html; https://www.solarwinds.com/blog/new-findings-from-our-investigation-of-sunburst). Every layer *except* build integrity held: the provenance/signature was genuine, and the source was never poisoned. XZ Utils: a two-year social-engineering campaign installed "Jia Tan" as maintainer; the backdoor lived not in the git tree but in the *release tarball* (a modified `build-to-host.m4` plus obfuscated binary test files activated only during deb/rpm builds), and it was signed by the legitimate maintainer (https://research.swtch.com/xz-timeline). Here source review *and* provenance both passed — the git tree was clean and the tarball was authentically signed — and no layer automatically compared the reviewed source to the shipped source. The backdoor was found by Andres Freund noticing a ~500 ms SSH latency and Valgrind errors, i.e. by an *individual human*, not by any of the cryptographic layers.

**Where the sources agree and where they are in tension.** There is near-unanimous **agreement** on the boundary the question names: reproducible-builds.org, the SLSA FAQ, the Arch paper, and Wheeler all independently state that correspondence is not benignity, and that a trusted seed or compiler is required somewhere. There is also agreement that verifier **independence** is load-bearing: SLSA warns rebuilders "must truly be independent, lest they all be susceptible to the same attack" (https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/faq); the Arch paper warns that even independent rebuilders fail together if "all of them are running the same compromised compiler… the final artifact can include malicious code while being completely reproducible" (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2505.21642). The **tensions** are structural. (1) Wheeler frames DDC as *detection* — a way to catch a subverted compiler — while the bootstrappable-builds lineage frames the attack as "only a symptom of an incomplete bootstrap" to be cured by shrinking the seed; two remedies, detection vs. elimination, that are complementary but rhetorically opposed. (2) SLSA/Sigstore anchor trust in the *build platform's signed attestations* and a centralized *identity* layer (IdPs, Fulcio root), whereas the R-B/B-B lineage anchors trust in *multiple rebuilders' outputs* and in *the reader auditing 357 bytes by hand* — a centralized-attestation model vs. a distributed-audit model with different failure modes. (3) SLSA explicitly declines transitivity and treats verified-reproducibility as *one optional* implementation, while the reproducible-builds/Guix/Bitcoin lineage treats 100% reproducibility as the *end goal itself* — Debian reaching ~95.2% of Bookworm/amd64 (75.8% for Arch, via rebuilderd), with none of the major distributions yet *enforcing* it (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2505.21642).

## Findings

**Claim.** End-to-end "verification" is a layered conjunction of *partial* proofs, each establishing exactly one invariant — [[Reproducible builds]] proves binary↔source correspondence, [[Software provenance]] (SLSA/Sigstore) proves origin and review *process*, [[Bootstrappable builds]] + [[Diverse double compilation]] minimize and cross-check the trusted compiler — and every layer's chain bottoms out in an irreducible trust residue (a seed binary, a trusted identity, trusted hardware). No layer, and no conjunction of layers, mechanically proves that the source itself is benign; that property is verified only by human review, which is exactly where the real attacks pass. The operative guarantee is therefore not a cryptographic proof but a *minimized, independently-held, failure-diverse* set of anchors: verification reduces risk multiplicatively across layers, it never reaches certainty.

**Grounds.** (a) Thompson shows correspondence cannot be established by reading source and benignity is a statement about people (https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rdriley/487/papers/Thompson_1984_ReflectionsonTrustingTrust.pdf). (b) Wheeler proves correspondence *is* mechanically decidable (DDC's formal proof) but concedes DDC does not establish benignity and still requires one trusted compiler (https://dwheeler.com/trusting-trust). (c) reproducible-builds.org and the SLSA FAQ both define reproducibility as correspondence and explicitly disclaim source/dependency/distribution threats ([[Reproducible builds (reproducible-builds.org)]], https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/faq). (d) Guix documents a minimized-but-nonzero residue: 357-byte seed + 25 MiB Guile driver, x86-only, firmware beneath (https://guix.gnu.org/en/blog/2023/the-full-source-bootstrap-building-from-source-all-the-way-down). (e) SLSA's source track attests process (two-party review, protected refs), not intent, and is non-transitive (https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/source-requirements, https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/threats). (f) SolarWinds passed provenance while the build step was poisoned — clean source, valid signature, dirty build (https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2023/solarwinds-explainer.html). (g) XZ passed review and provenance while the *shipped tarball diverged from the reviewed git tree* (https://research.swtch.com/xz-timeline). (h) Empirically, reproducibility is high but verifier independence is thin: ~95.2% Debian / 75.8% Arch, and even a fully reproducible package can be a fully reproducible backdoor (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2505.21642).

**Qualifier.** The claim holds for publicly-auditable FOSS supply chains at the current state of the art (2024–2026), and only if "verification" means *mechanical* proof. As a risk-reduction matter the conjunction is real and strong: each layer is a distinct barrier an attacker must cross, and the two canonical incidents each failed on exactly one layer, suggesting the layers are largely complementary rather than redundant. The claim is also bounded in scope: hardware/firmware root-of-trust is residual and unaddressed, and proprietary closed-source rebuilds are effectively excluded (the SolarWinds shape is uncatchable without an independent rebuilder, which closed source cannot grant).

**Rebuttals.** (1) *The residue is smaller than H1 claims.* The 357-byte seed is hex — a format the Guix authors call "readable as source" — so the bottom of the chain is not an "unauditable act of faith" but an artifact small enough for one person to hand-audit fully; the residue is minimized, not merely acknowledged. (2) *The benignity "gap" may be a category error, not a defect.* Benignity is semantic (intent), and no mechanism has ever claimed to prove intent; asking for a mechanical verifier of benignity is asking for the impossible, so the honest answer is that source benignity is and remains a social fact — "more important to trust the people who wrote the software" (Thompson). (3) *The independence thinness is an adoption gap, not an intrinsic limit.* Debian's ~95% is largely self-measured (distro test infrastructure rebuilding its own inputs, not third parties verifying shipped `.deb` files), and the Arch paper's own contribution — an independent rebuilder/verifier instance — shows the monoculture can be eroded one verifier at a time; with genuinely diverse rebuilders and toolchains the common-mode risk H3 worries about is reduced in practice.

## Open sub-questions

- What mechanism can automatically bind a *reviewed* source tree to the *shipped* artifact, so that a XZ-style tarball↔git divergence fails closed rather than silently? (Signed, git-derived release artifacts; reproducible *source* tarballs.)
- How independent are the existing rebuilders in practice, and what is the minimal set of failure-diverse rebuilders (different orgs, toolchains, ISAs) needed to defeat the common-mode compiler attack?
- Can the Guix 25 MiB `guile-bootstrap` driver be eliminated, and can the full-source bootstrap be ported to ARM/RISC-V without re-introducing an opaque GCC seed?
- Does SLSA's future Dependency track close the "compromised build tool" hole, and can it express the compiler-bootstrap threat (Thompson/DDC) rather than only packaging-level dependencies?
- How does the trust anchor shift when Sigstore's centralized IdP/Fulcio identity is replaced or augmented by decentralized keyless identity (e.g., git's own signed objects, TUF, or a transparency-log-only model)?
- What does the SolarWinds-shaped attack look like under a *mandatory* reproducibility regime (Debian 14 forky's policy), and can a proprietary vendor ever achieve the independence the layer requires?
