---
title: "Is it worth publishing the questions that come up?"
type: question
tags: ["research", "curiosity", "publishing", "ai"]
by: "Andy Smith"
status: "answered"
---

Yes — publish the questions, but make each one the doorway to a useful, evidence-backed research page rather than treating the raw question itself as the product. Your curiosity can interest people beyond you when it identifies a recognizable knowledge gap, gives readers enough context to feel that gap, and produces an answer they can inspect or use.

## Why the idea has real promise

**Questions can create interest before the answer arrives.** In trivia experiments, people were most curious when they had some knowledge but were uncertain of the answer; greater curiosity made them spend scarce tokens or waiting time to learn the answer and predicted better recall one to two weeks later ([[The wick in the candle of learning: epistemic curiosity activates reward circuitry and enhances memory (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov)|Kang et al.]]). This is direct evidence about the question–answer form, not about publishing a personal question list. The reasonable inference is that a well-framed question can recruit a reader's curiosity when it makes a meaningful, tractable gap visible. A question so private that outsiders lack context, or so broad that no answer seems reachable, will not automatically do this.

**A visible inquiry process is itself a legitimate publishing genre.** A study of 126 established research blogs found a working ecosystem in which individuals discussed peer-reviewed research: 72% had an active public Twitter account, and at least 90% of those accounts connected to another blogger in the sample ([[Research Blogs and the Discussion of Scholarly Information (journals.plos.org)|Shema, Bar-Ilan and Thelwall]]). The study demonstrates that public, source-linked learning can support a networked scholarly conversation. It does **not** measure whether readers prefer an author's curiosity, nor whether blogging caused reputation or collaboration.

The qualitative case is also strong. Andy Matuschak describes [[Work with the garage door up (notes.andymatuschak.org)|working with the garage door up]]: showing the problems, dead ends and daily work rather than only announcing finished products. He believes this creates a more invested following, but explicitly presents that as experience and suspicion, not controlled evidence. For an AI lab, this matters because the sequence of questions shows judgment — what you notice, how one uncertainty leads to another, and what you change your mind about. Generic answers are abundant; a coherent trajectory of inquiry is more distinctive. That final point is an inference, not a measured result.

**Interesting and useful material is more likely to travel.** In an observational study of every *New York Times* article published over three months, interestingness, surprise and practical usefulness were each positively associated with making the most-emailed list even after accounting for placement; accompanying experiments supported a causal role for high-arousal emotion in sharing ([[What Makes Online Content Viral? (doi.org)|Berger and Milkman]]). This does not prove that questions spread, but it argues against publishing an undifferentiated curiosity feed. Select and frame questions around a consequence, tension, surprise or decision that another person can recognize.

## The strongest objection: AI research can look useful while being wrong

A human evaluation of four generative search engines across 1,450 queries per system found high average ratings for fluency and perceived usefulness, while only 51.5% of generated statements were fully supported by citations and 74.5% of citations supported the associated statement ([[Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines (arxiv.org)|Liu, Zhang and Liang]]). The systems and results are from 2023, so the exact rates should not be projected onto current tools. The durable lesson is methodological: polished prose and attached links are not evidence of correctness.

That risk changes the publication design. Label AI as a research assistant, not an authority; link claims to primary sources; separate observations from inference; state uncertainty and counterevidence; show material corrections; and retain human editorial responsibility. Google’s publisher guidance makes the same practical distinction: automated content should disclose who made it, how it was made and why, and should add original value rather than merely summarize other sources ([[Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content (developers.google.com)|Google Search Central]]). This is platform guidance, not neutral proof of reader preference, but it is relevant to discoverability and trust.

## What to publish

Use the question as a durable organizing object, then earn attention with the answer:

1. **Lead with the question and stakes.** One sentence on why an outsider might care, what decision it informs, or what common assumption it tests.
2. **Publish a resolved page, not an AI transcript.** Give the current answer first; then evidence, uncertainty, competing explanations and practical implications.
3. **Expose provenance.** Cite the exact source behind each substantive claim, prefer primary evidence, disclose AI assistance, and distinguish evidence from your interpretation.
4. **Show the trail selectively.** Link follow-up questions and meaningful changes of mind. Do not publish every prompt, search query or weakly supported tangent.
5. **Let readers subscribe by theme.** A broad curiosity stream will be noisy; topic pages and tags let the right small audience find the right question.
6. **Measure usefulness, not applause.** Track qualified replies, citations, corrections, return readers and whether a page helps someone make a decision. Views alone cannot tell you whether the learning process is valuable.

## Decision

Run it as a bounded publishing experiment. Publish 10–20 question-led research pages in two or three themes, distribute each where people already discuss that problem, and invite corrections or follow-up questions. Continue if the pages produce repeated evidence of use — substantive replies, references, collaborations, or returning readers — and narrow the themes that do. The likely audience will be smaller than “everyone,” but that is not failure: a question only needs to be valuable to the people who share its knowledge gap.

The evidence supports the components — questions can induce curiosity, public research discourse exists, and useful or interesting content is more shareable — but no study found here directly tests an AI lab publishing all of its founder's questions. The recommendation is therefore a grounded inference with a cheap empirical test, not a guarantee of audience demand.
