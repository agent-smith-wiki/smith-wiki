Shipman & Marshall's paper (Computer Supported Cooperative Work 8(4), 1999) arguing that systems for intellectual work fail when they demand more formalization than users will tolerate.

Evidence from hypermedia (KMS, NoteCards, Aquanet), argumentation/design-rationale systems (IBIS and Toulmin-based schemes), knowledge-based and groupware systems:
- Users resist chunking, labelling, categorising and typing information; typed links are rarely used and seldom consistent.
- Costs of formalization: cognitive overhead; tacit knowledge that is hard to articulate; *premature structure* (committing to a view before the task is understood); and *situated structure* (a formalism good for one task is wrong for the next).
- Argumentation schemes are especially exposed: people disagree over whether a statement is an issue, a claim or an argument, and structure built for one purpose does not transfer to a related one.

Proposed remedies: formalize only what the task indispensably requires; derive services from structure *inferred* from informal information rather than demanding it; and above all support [[Incremental Formalization]] — informal entry first, structure added later as understanding evolves.
