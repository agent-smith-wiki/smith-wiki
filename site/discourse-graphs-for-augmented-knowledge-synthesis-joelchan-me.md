Chan's position paper defining a [[Discourse Graph]] as an information model representing scientific discourse as granular knowledge *claims* linked to supporting evidence and context in a graph.

Argument:
- Effective [[Knowledge Synthesis]] — integrating theories, concepts, claims and evidence into novel conceptual wholes — is critical to science but scarce and laborious, because the dominant information model is document-centric ("iTunes for papers").
- The unit that matters for synthesis is the claim, not the paper: questions like "which theories have empirical support?" and "where do predictions conflict?" are answered at the level of individual claims and their evidentiary context (measures, settings, samples, replication).
- Hypothesised benefits: for individuals, creative recombination and "decompression" of arguments; collectively, lower overhead and reuse of evidence collections across projects and people.
- Caveat: benefits are *hypothesised*, not shown. Deployments (ScholOnto, SWAN, micropublications, CIViC) were never empirically evaluated, and document-centric workflows still dominate.
- Opportunity: consumer "networked notebooks" (Roam, Obsidian, Logseq, ...) plus graph publishing make discourse graphs observable in authentic practice.

The node vocabulary derives from the [[Toulmin Argument Model]]; the warning about forcing structure comes from [[Formality Considered Harmful (people.engr.tamu.edu)|Formality Considered Harmful]].
