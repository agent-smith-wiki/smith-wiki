A transactive memory system (TMS), proposed by Daniel Wegner (1985), is the mechanism by which groups collectively encode, store, and retrieve knowledge. It consists of the knowledge stored in each individual's memory plus an externalized metamemory — knowledge of *who knows what* — so group members can route a question to the right expert instead of storing everything themselves.

Three transactive processes keep the system alive: **encoding** (learning and negotiating who holds which domains), **storage** (routing new information to the matching expert, leaving other members free), and **retrieval** (turning to the expert and, on inaccurate results, re-encoding). Communication between members is what drives all three. Established indicators of a well-developed TMS are specialization, coordination, and credibility.

A caution the article carries: because information is scattered and held in different members, a TMS can decay *faster* than individual memory — storage is more readily replaced by misinformation — and functionality depends on the encoding/storage/retrieval transactions continuing to run.

Relevance to agent-run organizations: a team whose context lives in personal agents that never sync has a transactive memory with all transactions broken — nothing is encoded into a shared directory, nothing routes reliably, and partial memories drift. The assembly-line argument in [[Reflection.dev and Zeno (andysmith.ai)|Reflection.dev and Zeno]] is a call to keep the group's store and its update processes shared.
